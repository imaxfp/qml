Complex Numbers — recommended topics
Topic	What to understand
Real part	\(a\) in \(z=a+bi\)
Imaginary part	\(b\) in \(z=a+bi\)
Complex conjugate	\(z^*=a-bi\)
Magnitude (modulus)	\(|z|=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\)
Phase (argument)	The angle of the complex number
Complex addition	Adding real and imaginary parts
Complex multiplication	How multiplication affects magnitude and phase
Polar form	\(z=r(\cos\theta+i\sin\theta)\)
Euler's formula	\(e^{i\theta}=\cos\theta+i\sin\theta\)
My recommended learning order:
1. Real and imaginary parts
2. Magnitude and phase
3. Complex conjugate
4. Polar form and Euler's formula
5. Complex multiplication
Why add conjugate and magnitude?

They are especially important in quantum computing:
- Magnitude squared (\(|z|^2\)) is used to calculate measurement probabilities.
- Complex conjugate is used in inner products, which help calculate overlaps between quantum states.
You don't need to study complex analysis. These fundamentals are enough to move toward qubits and quantum gates.

2. Complex Vectors
Topic	What to understand
Vectors basics	What a vector is and how to represent it
Complex-valued vectors	Vectors whose components are complex numbers
Vector addition	Adding vectors component by component
Scalar multiplication	Multiplying a vector by a complex number
Vector magnitude (norm)	Measuring the length of a complex vector
Complex conjugate of a vector	Applying conjugation to each component
Inner product (dot product)	Measuring the relationship/overlap between two vectors
Normalization	Making a vector's norm equal to 1

Why is this important for quantum computing?

A pure qubit state is represented by a normalized complex vector:
\[
|\psi\rangle =
\begin{bmatrix}
\alpha\\
\beta
\end{bmatrix}
\]

where \(\alpha,\beta\) are complex numbers.

For now, focus especially on complex-valued vectors, norm, conjugate, inner product, and normalization. These concepts will directly prepare you to understand qubit states.

Recommended learning roadmap
1. Complex numbers and linear algebra
   
   Understand the mathematical language.
2. Single qubit
   
   Understand superposition, probability, phase and measurement.
3. Quantum gates
   
   Understand how quantum states change.
4. Multiple qubits
   
   Understand tensor products and entanglement.
5. Quantum circuits
   
   Combine gates into algorithms.
6. QML
   
   Build parameterized circuits, quantum kernels and hybrid models.

My recommendation: Don't rush into quantum algorithms yet.

First, understand these five concepts particularly well:
1. Complex amplitudes.
2. Superposition.
3. Quantum phase.
4. Measurement and probability.
5. Quantum gates (matrix × vector).
Once these click, the rest of quantum computing becomes much easier to understand.

We can study each concept with mathematical examples and Python simulations, building from a single qubit toward QML.
